## The RGB cube

Every hex colour is three numbers, red, green and blue, each from 0 to 255. Put them on three axes and the colours fill a cube: black at one corner, white at the opposite one, pure red, green and blue along the three edges that leave black. Drag to turn it, scroll or pinch to zoom, and narrow each channel to a range, from its low handle to its high one, to see inside; *Select all* opens all three to 0–255, *Select none* hides every sample. The lattice drawn over it marks the eight corners, from black and white to the pure primaries and their mixes cyan, magenta and yellow, the twelve edge midpoints, where two channels sit at 0 or 255 and the third halfway, from maroon #800000 to light yellow #ffff80, the six face centres, where one channel sits at 0 or 255 and the other two halfway, plus mid grey #808080 at the very centre: together the 27 points form the 3×3×3 lattice of channel values 0, 128 and 255, each joined by a line to its neighbours one step along a single channel, 54 lines in all. The centres of the eight small cubes it cuts out, every channel at 64 or 192, from dark grey #404040 to silver #c0c0c0, are joined to nothing, and the two greys among them are drawn smaller; the six tertiaries' shades, each half-way from its hue to black, from #804000 to #800040, their six tints, each half-way to white, from #ffc080 to #ff80c0, and their six tones, each half-way to mid grey, from #c08040 to #c04080, are joined to nothing too, and so are all twelve hues' light tones, each half-way to silver #c0c0c0, from #e06060 to #e060a0, and their dark tones, each half-way to dark grey #404040, from #a02020 to #a02060, and the tones of all twelve hues' tints, each half-way from the tint to mid grey #808080, from #c08080 to #c080a0, and of their shades, each half-way from the shade to mid grey, from #804040 to #804060; the light and dark tones and the tertiaries' tones of tints and shades are the only points off the 5×5×5 grid of 0, 64, 128, 192 and 255: 101 points in all. The lines are all one muted colour except those that leave black or white: the three cube edges from black to red, lime and blue and the shade rays are black, the three cube edges from white to yellow, cyan and magenta and the tint rays are white. The grey axis between them is black from black to mid grey #808080 and white from there to white. Click a point to focus it, and everything else fades; *On click* chooses what stays with it. *Grid lines* keeps the three lattice lines through it, one per channel, with every point on them right across the cube, 7 points in all for red — its runs to black, to yellow and to magenta; a small cube's centre, a tertiary's shade, tint or tone, a light or dark tone, or the tone of a tint or a shade is on no line, so it stays alone. *Hue family* keeps its hue's family: the hue, its tint, light tone, tone, dark tone and shade and the tones of its tint and its shade, the five greys they are mixed with, white, silver #c0c0c0, mid grey #808080, dark grey #404040 and black, the five rays out of those greys through each half-way mix to the hue, each in its grey's colour, and the two mid-grey lines out of mid grey through the tones of its tint and its shade to the tint and the shade, 13 points in all; a mix brings up its hue's family, so maroon #800000 keeps the same as red, and a grey keeps the five neutrals and the grey axis. Either way the hint in the corner of the view stays on the clicked colour while the pointer is elsewhere, and says what it is: *Primary · pure hue*, *Primary · shade (red + black)*, *Tertiary · light tone (orange + #c0c0c0)*, *Primary · tone of shade (navy + #808080)*, *Neutral*. Click it again or empty space to clear.

*Show all*, *Shade all* and *Hide all* set the whole lattice, and the *Highlight* table draws chosen groups of points in full colour on top, so under *Shade all* they stand out from a faded lattice and under *Hide all* they stand alone. The table sorts every colour by two questions at once. Its columns ask what a hue is mixed with, and hang in three groups, each off the colour its family of tones starts from: under *Pure hue*, the *Pure* hue at the cube's full reach, one channel at 0 and one at 255, then its *Light tone*, *Tone* and *Dark tone*, mixed with a light grey, mid grey and a dark grey; under *Tint*, the *Tint*, mixed with white, and the *Tone of tint*, that tint mixed in turn with mid grey; under *Shade*, the *Shade*, mixed with black, and the *Tone of shade*, that shade mixed with mid grey. Each named hue's tint, light tone, tone, dark tone and shade are its half-way mixes with white, silver #c0c0c0, mid grey #808080, dark grey #404040 and black, one point each, and each sits in the column of the grey it was mixed with, not wherever its lightness would put it: red's are #ff8080, #e06060, #c04040, #a02020 and #800000, orange's #ffc080, #e0a060, #c08040, #a06020 and #804000. The tones of its tint and its shade are those two mixed half-way again with mid grey: red's #c08080 and #804040, orange's #c0a080 and #806040. Its rows ask which hue, by its angle round the wheel: *Primaries* at multiples of 120°, red, lime and blue; *Secondaries* at the other multiples of 60°, yellow, cyan and magenta; and *Tertiaries* at the six multiples of 30° between, orange, chartreuse, spring green, azure, violet and rose. The twelve named hues run as a ring round the six edges that touch neither black nor white: the colour wheel of a screen. These are common wheel names, standardised by no body — chartreuse and spring green come from the X11/CSS list, the rest are common usage — a vocabulary worth knowing, though the diagram labels its points by the standard systems below rather than by it. The greys on the diagonal from black to white have no hue and sit outside the table, under *Neutrals*, joined by a line when it is on, so each of the 101 points is in exactly one place. Each cell highlights exactly its own points and shows how many of the 101 there are, and an empty one is greyed out; a row or column heading toggles the whole row or column, a group heading all the columns under it, and whatever is on adds up. The primaries and the secondaries hold 3 points in each column, the tertiaries 6 pure hues, their 6 tints, #ffc080, #c0ff80, #80ffc0, #80c0ff, #c080ff and #ff80c0, their 6 tones, #c08040, #80c040, #40c080, #4080c0, #8040c0 and #c04080, and their 6 shades, #804000, #408000, #008040, #004080, #400080 and #800040, and the neutrals 5: by column, 12 pure hues, 12 light tones, 12 tones, 12 dark tones, 12 tints, 12 tones of tints, 12 shades and 12 tones of shades, every one labelled. The light tones are #e06060, #60e060 and #6060e0 for the primaries, #e0e060, #60e0e0 and #e060e0 for the secondaries, and #e0a060, #a0e060, #60e0a0, #60a0e0, #a060e0 and #e060a0 for the tertiaries; the dark tones #a02020, #20a020 and #2020a0, #a0a020, #20a0a0 and #a020a0, and #a06020, #60a020, #20a060, #2060a0, #6020a0 and #a02060; the tones of tints #c08080, #80c080 and #8080c0, #c0c080, #80c0c0 and #c080c0, and #c0a080, #a0c080, #80c0a0, #80a0c0, #a080c0 and #c080a0; and the tones of shades #804040, #408040 and #404080, #808040, #408080 and #804080, and #806040, #608040, #408060, #406080, #604080 and #804060; the primaries' and secondaries' six tones and the two middle greys are the small cubes' centres. With the pure hues of *Primaries* and *Secondaries* both on, a line joins each secondary to the two primaries it mixes, magenta to red and blue, cyan to green and blue, yellow to red and green: the six lines are the ring's edges, and each tertiary sits halfway along one. A tint is a hue mixed toward white, a light tone, tone or dark tone one mixed toward silver, mid grey or dark grey, and a shade one mixed toward black, so each sits on the straight line between the two, and every mix cell that is on draws those lines for its own hues, each ray in the colour of the grey or corner it leaves: a white ray out of the white corner through each tint, a silver one out of silver #c0c0c0 through each light tone, a mid-grey one out of mid grey #808080 through each tone, a dark-grey one out of dark grey #404040 through each dark tone and a black one out of black through each shade, each running on to its hue. *Secondaries* × *Tone* draws three, through #c0c040, #40c0c0 and #c040c0 to yellow, cyan and magenta, and a whole column all twelve; for *Primaries* × *Shade*, maroon, green and navy, the rays are the three cube edges from black to red, lime and blue; *Tertiaries* × *Shade* draws six black rays, each out of black through a tertiary's shade to the tertiary, *Tertiaries* × *Tint* six white ones out of white through each tint, *Tertiaries* × *Tone* six mid-grey ones out of mid grey #808080 through each tone, and *Primaries* × *Light tone* three silver ones, through #e06060, #60e060 and #6060e0 to red, lime and blue, and *Primaries* × *Dark tone* three dark-grey ones, through #a02020, #20a020 and #2020a0. The tone of a tint or a shade lies on the line from mid grey to that tint or shade, and its cell draws that line, mid grey like the tone rays: *Primaries* × *Tone of shade* three, out of mid grey through #804040, #408040 and #404080 to maroon, green and navy, and *Tertiaries* × *Tone of tint* six, through #c0a080 to #ffc080 and the rest. In OKLCH the same mixes bend, so there the rays curve. Clicking a point still focuses it, with its lines or its family as *On click* says, whatever the table holds, the rest faded, or hidden under *Hide all*; clearing the focus returns to the highlight. *Colour names* chooses which standard naming a label uses, or *None* for no labels at all, the tooltip still naming a point under the pointer: *Hex* the hex code, *CSS* the nearest CSS colour keyword, marked `≈` where it is only near — exact keywords are few, 16 of these 101 colours, HTML's sixteen basic ones — *HWB* the colour as CSS Color 4 writes it in `hwb()`, its hue in degrees and then how much white and how much black, navy `hwb(240 0% 50%)`, *Munsell* the renotation string, hue, value and chroma as `7.8R 5.2/20.4`, neutrals written `N 5.3/` with no hue, and *ISCC–NBS* the systematic name from the ISCC–NBS dictionary, which covers every colour and says it in plain terms, light greenish yellow and the like. HWB reads a colour as a hue plus how much white and how much black are in it: a tint has only white, a shade only black and a tone both, the same split as the table's columns.

HWB also says what a tone of a tint or a shade keeps. Mid grey #808080 is `hwb(0 50% 50%)`, half white and half black, so a tint, which already holds its half of white, keeps it all the way to mid grey and only gains black: blue's tint #8080ff, `hwb(240 50% 0%)`, goes half-way to #8080c0, `hwb(240 50% 25%)`, on a line of equal whiteness. A shade keeps its half of black and only gains white: navy #000080, `hwb(240 0% 50%)`, goes half-way to #404080, `hwb(240 25% 50%)`, on a line of equal blackness. So each family of tones hangs off the colour it starts from, and the table groups its columns that way: the pure hue with the tones mixed straight from it, the tint with the tone of the tint, the shade with the tone of the shade. *Tone of tint* and *Tone of shade* hold 12 points each, 3 for each primary and secondary row and 6 for the tertiaries, the 24 that bring the lattice from 77 points to 101.

## The hue wheel

*Wheel* is the cube seen down its grey diagonal, from white straight through to black (*Look down the grey axis* turns the cube there), with that depth collapsed: black, white and every grey fall on the centre, lightness is gone and only hue is left, so the hexagon of the six coloured corners rounds off into a circle. On this RGB wheel the primaries are the three lights a screen mixes from, red, lime and blue, ringed twice; the secondaries are two primaries in equal part, yellow, cyan and magenta; and the tertiaries, ringed thinnest, are each a primary mixed with the secondary beside it: orange, chartreuse, spring green, azure, violet and rose, 30° apart all round. The painter's RYB wheel has other primaries, red, yellow and blue, and calls chartreuse yellow-green. Inside the rim, seven more rings of twelve are the *Highlight* table's other columns, from the pure hues at the rim inward through the tints, light tones, tones of tints, tones, tones of shades and dark tones to the shades, each dot on its hue's spoke, and the five neutrals, which have no hue, stack at the centre, white over black. The tone of a tint has its light tone's HSL lightness, half-way between its highest and lowest channel, at half the chroma, and the tone of a shade its dark tone's, so each rings just inside its twin, on the side of the greyer tone. The table works as in the cube and carries across: with nothing chosen every ring shows, otherwise the chosen cells keep full strength and the rest fade; hover a dot inside the rim for its name under *Colour names*, as for a hue on the rim whose long name would run into its neighbour's on a narrow screen, and *None* leaves the wheel with no names at all, not even the hues', the hint still naming the colour under the pointer. Click any dot to keep its name and its hint and to focus its family, as *Hue family* does in the cube: the eight dots on its hue's spoke, or the five neutrals, keep full strength and the rest fade, until it or empty space is clicked again.

## sRGB in OKLCH

The [RGB colour cube](#the-rgb-cube) is even in numbers, not in what we see. OKLCH is a colour space built for the eye: **L** is perceived lightness from black (0) to white (1), **C** is chroma, how far a colour stands from grey, and **H** is hue, an angle around the circle. Here the cube is sampled and every sample is moved to its OKLCH place, painted in its own colour: lightness up, chroma outward from the grey axis, hue around it.

The result is lumpy because equal chroma is not reachable at every hue and lightness in sRGB. Yellow is bright by nature, so it peaks high, close to white; blue is dark, so it reaches far out low, near black; the reds and magentas bulge in between, and no screen colour fills the gaps. HSL would not show this: it is only the cube reshaped into a cylinder, every hue stretched to the same full saturation whether the eye agrees or not. Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom, and narrow lightness, chroma and hue to look inside.

The vertical line is the lightness axis, the greys from black to white. The ring at its foot marks hue, labelled where sRGB's six pure colours land, and the fainter circles mark chroma 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3. Grey samples have no hue, so the hue cut leaves them in place. *Look from above* flattens lightness away and shows the hue wheel with its uneven reach.

The cube's 3×3×3 lattice of channel values 0, 128 and 255 comes over too, with the eight small-cube centres, the six tertiaries' shades, tints and tones and every hue's light and dark tone and the tones of its tint and shade, 101 points, with each neighbour line bent to pass through the colours between its ends; as in the cube, click a point to focus it with its lines or its family, as *On click* says, and click again or on empty space to clear. Evenly spaced in RGB, the lattice is stretched and bent here: magenta's corner reaches farthest from the grey axis, blue's close behind, the first step out of black toward green, to #008000, is more than six times as long as the step from lime #00ff00 to chartreuse #80ff00, and the steps along the light greens, cyans and yellows are the shortest of all. *Show all*, *Shade all*, *Hide all*, the *Highlight* table and *Colour names* work as in the cube; the lines joining each secondary to its two primaries bend here like the rest.

## moene's grid colour field inside the cube

Switch on *moene grid* (and off *Show samples* to see it alone) to plot the 81 colours of the [grid colour field](/grid-colour-field/), moene's 9×9 mood × energy grid, each at its own place in the cube. The nine big ringed points are the region colours, from deep navy *depressive state* to white at the top of neutral mood; lines join subcells that are neighbours on the grid. The flat grid becomes a folded sheet through colour space: its edges run out to the cube's faces, where the most intense colours were clipped. The palette is fixed, moene's own nine colours.

## The capsule wardrobe palette inside the cube

Switch on *Capsule wardrobe* (and off *Show samples* to see it alone) to plot the 16 colour families of the smart-casual capsule wardrobe, each a ringed point named after its family. Most of them huddle in the dark, low-saturation corner near black and along the grey diagonal: browns, olives, greys and navy, with white, off-white and light blue the only colours up near white. As in the field, lines join them into chains: the neutrals from white through the greys to black, then light blue to navy, light to dark brown (rusty and taupe hang off light brown, their nearest brown) and light to dark olive, each chromatic chain tied at its dark end to graphite, its nearest neutral. Names follow *Axes and labels*. Click a palette colour to show its shades, a cloud of small dots joined to it by faint spokes, and click it again to hide them. The clouds cover taupe, ochre, rust, the off-whites (ivory, ecru, cream…), the browns (beige, sand, camel, chocolate…) and the denim washes; they and ochre, linked from rusty to light olive, are illustrative extras for this page, display-only and not part of the inventory vocabulary.

In OKLCH both overlays move with everything else: the capsule's browns, olives and greys sit close to the lightness axis, low in chroma and spread mostly up and down, and the field's folded sheet bends the same way.

## Is that every colour there is?

No. The cube holds every colour a screen can show in standard sRGB: 256 × 256 × 256, about 16.7 million. Human vision reaches further.

- **The most saturated colours lie outside it.** Laser green, deep cyan, deep violet and the pure colours of a rainbow cannot be mixed from a screen's red, green and blue. sRGB covers roughly a third of the colours a person can see; wider screen spaces (Display P3, Rec. 2020) make the cube bigger.
- **Colour happens in the eye.** Light is a whole spectrum of wavelengths, which the eye reduces to three signals. Different spectra can look identical, and some animals see colours we cannot.
- **Equal steps in the cube do not look equal.** The cube is even in numbers, not in perception, which is why perceptual spaces such as OKLab exist; the [same colours in OKLCH](#srgb-in-oklch) stop being a cube.

So the cube is the screen's world of colour: a slice of what we can see, not all of it.
